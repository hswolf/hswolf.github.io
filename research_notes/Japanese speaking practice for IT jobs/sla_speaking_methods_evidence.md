## 1. Theories: how speaking develops, and the role of input vs. output

### Takeaway
All five frameworks agree that comprehension (input) is necessary but does not build speaking on its own. Speaking is a separate, skill-specific system (Levelt/Kormos; DeKeyser). It is built through production with feedback (Swain; Long) and then sped up through fluency work on familiar material (Nation). Meta-analyses support this: interaction and feedback produce durable gains, and production-based practice beats comprehension-only practice on delayed tests of *productive* knowledge.

### Cited Findings

**Swain: Output Hypothesis** (theory, 1985/1995; grew out of research on L2-French immersion in Canada)
- Origin. Swain developed the hypothesis partly as a response to input-only views, from her work with Canadian French-immersion students, who had extensive comprehensible input. She argued that *producing* language forces learners to attend to morphosyntax — [Wikipedia: Comprehensible output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)
- The three functions of output:
  - **Noticing**: the learner notices the gap between what they want to say and what they can say.
  - **Hypothesis testing**: an utterance is a "trial run" of how to say something, checked against the listener's reaction or feedback.
  - **Metalinguistic/reflective**: learners talk or think about their own language use.
  - Sources — [Byrne (UCLan)](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/12157/1/12157_byrne.pdf); [JALT 2001 Proceedings](https://jalt-publications.org/archive/proceedings/2001/579.pdf)
- **"Pushed output"** means learners must be stretched to produce more precise, coherent and accurate language. Secondary reviews call the experimental support mixed but suggestive. Learners modified their output more often, and more successfully, when pushed. However, many studies did not show a clear noticing effect — [JALT 2001 Proceedings](https://jalt-publications.org/archive/proceedings/2001/579.pdf); [Byrne (UCLan)](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/12157/1/12157_byrne.pdf)
- An early direct experimental test is Izumi, Bigelow, Fujiwara & Fearnow (1999), "Testing the output hypothesis" (SSLA; L2 English). I did not retrieve its results — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/testing-the-output-hypothesis/8437C53035A5FD15BD985463E5428B36); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/testing-the-output-hypothesis/)

**Long: Interaction Hypothesis** (negotiation of meaning and feedback in conversation)
- 2007 · meta-analysis · mixed L2s, mostly English. Mackey & Goo pooled 28 interaction studies. Interaction produced d = 0.71 on immediate post-tests and d = 1.09 on delayed post-tests. Gains grew rather than faded (secondary) — [Lancaster record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/59863/); figures via [McGill-hosted CF paper](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- By setting, classroom studies gave d = 0.57 immediate and 0.76 delayed, against d = 0.96 and 1.20 for laboratory studies. The sources don't make clear whether this split belongs to Mackey & Goo's classroom subset or to Lyster & Saito's summary (secondary) — [McGill-hosted CF paper](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- 1998 · two experiments · **★JFL** and L2 Spanish. Long, Inagaki & Ortega (MLJ 82(3), 357–371) compared models (correct examples heard in advance) with recasts (reformulations after an error). Recasts beat models for short-term gains on one previously unseen structure. A citing paper places that result in the Spanish adverb-placement target. I did not confirm the Japanese experiment's result — [catalog record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_58373790); [scite listing](https://scite.ai/authors/shunji-inagaki-dvwJg9)
- 2003 · experiment · **★JFL**. Iwashita (SSLA 25(1), 1–36) studied 55 beginning learners of Japanese at an Australian university in task-based interaction with native speakers, targeting two Japanese structures.
  - Positive evidence (correct models) was about 10 times more frequent than implicit negative feedback, but only learners who scored above average on the pretest benefited from it.
  - Implicit negative feedback helped short-term development *regardless* of initial mastery.
  - Recasts had a larger short-term effect than the other conversational moves.
  - Source — [Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/negative-feedback-and-positive-evidence-in-taskbased-interaction/7A1B2BADFD35078D677D98C6D81569B6)

**DeKeyser: Skill Acquisition Theory** (theory; ACT-R-based)
- L2 learning is treated as skill learning. Declarative knowledge (explicit facts) becomes procedural knowledge (skill-specific routines) and then automatized performance through practice — [DeKeyser & Criado 2013](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142072/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Automatization_Skill%20Acquisition%20and%20Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Acquisition_2013.pdf); [Cambridge core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DF879921EDE594E795CBD8C18A87E86E/core-reader)
- Refinement (DeKeyser 2015): declarative knowledge causally *supports* procedural development without necessarily being *converted* into it — [Oba 2019 overview](https://core.ac.uk/download/276551405.pdf)
- **Practice effects are skill-specific**, and the declarative/procedural distinction explains why. Comprehension practice mainly builds comprehension, and production practice builds production — [DeKeyser (ed.) 2007, *Practice in a Second Language*, introduction excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/84040/excerpt/9780521684040_excerpt.pdf)
- Rival accounts exist, including Logan's instance theory and race models — [Cambridge core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DF879921EDE594E795CBD8C18A87E86E/core-reader)

**Levelt's speech-production model, Kormos's L2 adaptation, and Segalowitz's fluency framework** (theory)
- Levelt's model has three stages:
  - The **conceptualizer** plans the message.
  - The **formulator** chooses words and grammar and encodes the sounds.
  - The **articulator** produces the speech.
  - Self-monitoring runs throughout.
  - Sources — [Language Teaching, "Oral fluency in a second language: a research agenda"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/oral-fluency-in-a-second-language-a-research-agenda-for-the-next-ten-years/9C05358121F94757BE14049B2F4887FC/share/5db835c18522e3a8198bdc85d4e4de26c9352d34); [Leiden thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2976055/download)
- Kormos (2006) adapts the model to L2. The L2 formulator runs more slowly on declarative L2 knowledge until that knowledge is automatized — [Language Teaching research agenda](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/oral-fluency-in-a-second-language-a-research-agenda-for-the-next-ten-years/9C05358121F94757BE14049B2F4887FC/share/5db835c18522e3a8198bdc85d4e4de26c9352d34)
- Segalowitz (2010) distinguishes three kinds of fluency:
  - **Cognitive fluency** is how efficiently the production system runs.
  - **Utterance fluency** is the measurable timing of speech: speed, pauses, repairs.
  - **Perceived fluency** is listeners' judgments.
  - Source — [Leiden thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2976055/download)

**Nation: Four Strands** (theory/curriculum principle, 2007)
- The four strands are meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning, and fluency development. A course should give them "roughly equal" time — [Nation 2007, "The Four Strands" (core.ac.uk copy)](https://core.ac.uk/download/357330216.pdf)
- Language-focused learning should take no more than about a quarter of the time — [Wikipedia: Paul Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nation)
- The fluency-development strand is about making the best use of what the learner *already knows*, receiving and producing it at a reasonable rate — [Nation 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/357330216.pdf)
- A 2024 discussion paper observes that the four-strands balance is rarely achieved in practice (attribution from a search summary) — [VNU Journal of Foreign Studies](https://jfs.ulis.vnu.edu.vn/index.php/fs/article/download/5201/4525)

**Input vs. output compared directly**
- 2013 · meta-analysis · mixed L2s. Shintani, Li & Ellis (Language Learning 63(2), 296–329) pooled 35 projects from 30 studies that compared comprehension-based instruction (CBI) with production-based instruction (PBI) for grammar.
  - Both had large effects overall.
  - For receptive knowledge, CBI beat PBI on tests within a week, but the gap shrank on delayed tests.
  - For productive knowledge, the two were similar in the short term, and **PBI was better on delayed tests**.
  - CBI's early advantage came mainly from Processing Instruction.
  - Source — [Curtin repository](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/54469)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis for the report.** Input-only plans ("just listen a lot") are not supported for building speaking. Every major theory and the CBI/PBI meta-analysis point to production practice as necessary, with feedback (Swain, Long) and repetition toward automaticity (DeKeyser). Input remains essential: it is one of the four strands, and Iwashita shows models help learners who already know more.
- **Diagnosis for this learner** (N4–N3, preparing for the JLPT, no daily speaking). JLPT study is almost entirely language-focused learning plus reading and listening input. In Nation's terms, the two output-side strands, meaning-focused output and fluency development, are probably near zero. In Levelt/Kormos terms, the learner likely has a lot of *declarative* grammar and vocabulary that is not yet *proceduralized* in the formulator. That fits the common profile of passing N3 but freezing in conversation.
- How each theory maps to a practice type:
  - Swain and Long → tasks with a real listener plus feedback (tutor, AI).
  - DeKeyser → repeated, spaced, increasingly fast production of the same content and structures (task repetition, 4/3/2, productive retrieval).
  - Levelt → reduce load on the conceptualizer by using familiar content and planning time, so attention can go to formulating Japanese.
  - Nation → the time budget across the four strands (see Section 5).
- **JFL-specific signal.** Iwashita 2003 suggests that at lower levels, conversational feedback on errors (recasts) helps regardless of level. Simply hearing correct Japanese helps mainly the stronger learners. That argues for conversation practice with feedback, not shadowing or listening alone.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the original texts of Long (1996) and Swain (1985/1995). The descriptions above rely on secondary summaries.
- I could not confirm the results of the Japanese experiment in Long, Inagaki & Ortega (1998), only that the Spanish result favored recasts.
- No study was found that directly tests the four-strands time split. The "equal time" principle is a curriculum principle, not an experimental finding.
- Many hosts blocked fetching, and the search budget ran out (see the method note at the top).

## 2. Which speaking techniques have empirical support, and how strong is it?

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is for oral corrective feedback, interaction, explicit pronunciation instruction, planning time, and spacing, each with meta-analyses and mostly medium effects. The most consistent finding for repetition techniques (task repetition, 4/3/2) is that they make speech faster, while the effects on accuracy and complexity depend on how the repetition is set up. Shadowing, self-transcription, formulaic-sequence training, reading aloud, strategy training and private speech rest on small studies or descriptive work, so they are plausible supplements rather than proven core methods.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Oral corrective feedback (CF): recasts vs. prompts
Definitions: a **recast** reformulates the learner's error correctly. A **prompt** pushes the learner to self-correct; prompts include elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic clues and repetition of the error.
- 1997 · observational · L2 French immersion, children in Grades 4–5. Lyster & Ranta coded teacher feedback in four classrooms.
  - Recasts were 55% of all feedback, elicitation 14%, clarification requests 11%, metalinguistic feedback 8%, explicit correction 7%, and repetition 5%.
  - Recasts were the most common type but the least likely to lead to student-generated repair.
  - Only about 27% of feedback moves led to repair (secondary).
  - Sources — [McGill eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/0p096b851); [CARLA archive](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol1/Bridge1.3.pdf)
- 2006 · meta-analysis. Russell & Spada found d = 1.16 for CF overall (secondary; pools laboratory and classroom studies) — [McGill-hosted CF paper](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- 2010 · meta-analysis · mixed L2s. Li (Language Learning 60(2), 309–365) covered 22 articles and 11 dissertations.
  - The overall CF effect was **medium and maintained over time**.
  - Explicit feedback had larger immediate effects, but implicit feedback's effects lasted longer.
  - Laboratory studies gave larger effects than classroom studies.
  - The commonly cited overall d = 0.61 was *not* confirmed in this session.
  - Source — [Auckland repository](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/24985?show=full)
- 2010 · meta-analysis, classroom studies only · mixed L2s and ages. Lyster & Saito (SSLA 32, 265–302) pooled 15 quasi-experimental studies (N = 827).
  - CF was effective and durable.
  - **Prompts were more effective than recasts**, most clearly on free, constructed-response measures. One summary describes medium effects for recasts and large effects for prompts.
  - **Younger learners benefited more than older ones.**
  - Source — [Cambridge (SSLA)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- 2006 · quasi-experimental classroom study · L2 English. Ammar & Spada found prompts more effective overall. Whether recasts worked depended on learner proficiency — [PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Ahlem-Ammar-2/publication/232021519_One_size_fits_all_Recasts_prompts_and_L2_learning/links/590885a0458515ebb49088ac/One-size-fits-all-Recasts-prompts-and-L2-learning.pdf)
- 2020 · meta-analysis · Chinese learners of English. Schenck found the effect depends heavily on the grammatical feature targeted. **Recasts beat prompts for more semantically or syntactically complex features** — [Springer (APJSFLE)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9.pdf)
- 2016 · meta-analysis. Brown (Language Teaching Research) examined CF type and linguistic focus. It exists, but I did not retrieve its findings — [BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100066197262_0x000001)
- 2023 · systematic review. Xu & Zeng looked at the timing of CF. Few studies compare immediate and delayed CF, and they show **no clear difference**. Spacing and reconsolidation theories predict an advantage for delayed CF, while immediate CF imposes more cognitive load — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1026174/full)
- **★JFL.** Iwashita 2003 found recasts gave the largest short-term effect among conversational moves (see Section 1). Ohta 2001 (see section K) documents how JFL learners privately process recasts addressed to classmates — [LINGUIST List review](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/linguist/2001-May/013397.html)
- **Grade: Strong** that CF works, mostly for grammatical accuracy, with effects lasting to delayed tests. **Moderate** that prompts beat recasts, since the evidence is mostly classroom-based, children are over-represented, and the result depends on the target feature and the learner's proficiency.

#### B. Task repetition, including the 4/3/2 technique
- 2011 · experiment · L2 English (high-intermediate ESL). de Jong & Perfetti (Language Learning 61(2), 533–568) tested 4/3/2: the same talk given in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. The group that repeated the *same* topic made greater fluency gains on a post-test than a comparison group. The authors attribute this to proceduralization (secondary project summary) — [LearnLab project page](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fluency_Summer_Intern_Project_2010); [LearnLab: Fostering fluency](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning)
- 2016 · experiment · L2 English · **VN-L1**. Thai & Boers (TESOL Quarterly 50, 369–393) had 20 Vietnamese EFL students give the same talk three times.
  - Under **shrinking time** (4/3/2), fluency gains were largest, but complexity and accuracy did not change.
  - Under **constant time**, fluency gains were smaller but came with **modest complexity and accuracy gains**.
  - Learners reused much of their wording verbatim, especially under time pressure.
  - Sources — [record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/tesq.232); [citing paper (UCL, LTR 2021)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10128571/1/LTR2021.pdf)
- 2025 · practitioner article. When 4/3/2 shrinks the time, linguistic complexity and accuracy may suffer — [English Teaching Forum (US State Dept.)](https://americanenglish.state.gov/files/ae/resource_files/63_3_p02-08.pdf)
- 2025 · experiment · L2 English, 52 Polish learners. Róg (Language Teaching) found that task repetition increased lexical diversity and marginally improved fluency. Task enjoyment did not change these effects — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-between-task-repetition-and-oral-task-enjoyment-effects-on-speech-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency/D8C1FD395F5275FF3F3AC2356FB15A45)
- Reviews:
  - Dawadi (2019, ITL): "considerable research … but the results are not yet conclusive" — [Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/itl.16025.daw)
  - A review in a System paper: repetition reliably helps fluency, with more variable results for complexity and accuracy — [White Rose (System)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/139941/2/System_FinalV.pdf)
- Systematic review by Rogers of oral task-repetition studies from 1996 to 2023, focused on the **time interval between repetitions** as an under-reported variable. I did not retrieve its findings — [PolyU repository](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/113277/1/Rogers_Time_Between_Tasks.pdf)
- **No speaking-specific meta-analysis of task repetition was found.** The one task-repetition meta-analysis found (Liu & Tang) is on L2 *writing*, where repetition gave g = 0.51 for writing fluency — [PDF](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-12/LTR-effect-of-task-repetition-meta-analysis.pdf)
- 2024 · meta-analysis of task sequencing. Abdi Tabari, Johnson & Farahanynia (IRAL) found that more complex tasks yielded larger effect sizes — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2024-0219/pdf)
- Bygate's 2018 edited volume, *Learning Language through Task Repetition*, includes chapters on transfer to *new tasks of the same type*, on stimulated-recall awareness-raising between repetitions, and on "task repetition vs. task iteration". Lynch & Maclean (2000, LTR 4(3), 221–250) is the classic study of repetition and recycling in a classroom — [Benjamins chapter](https://benjamins.com/catalog/tblt.11.08lyn)
- **Grade: Moderate** for fluency gains on repeated content. **Limited or mixed** for accuracy and complexity gains and for transfer to new tasks.

#### C. Pre-task (strategic) and within-task (online) planning
- 2022 · meta-analysis · 36 studies, mostly L2 English (the year and the language mix are from my prior knowledge; the sources retrieved here don't state them). Johnson & Abdi Tabari (Applied Linguistics):
  - **Pre-task planning** had **medium effects on syntactic complexity and fluency** and **small positive effects on accuracy**.
  - **Within-task planning**, meaning unpressured performance without a time limit, had **large positive effects on syntactic complexity and accuracy**.
  - The results offer some support for Robinson's Cognition Hypothesis.
  - Figures come from a secondary summary — [ECU news item](https://english.ecu.edu/?p=13678); [Bohrium summary](https://www.bohrium.com/en/paper-details/task-planning-and-oral-l2-production-a-research-synthesis-and-meta-analysis/865005257623601348-9980)
- 2017 · research synthesis. Suzuki (University of Hawai'i Second Language Studies) finds that past studies support planning, "especially in terms of fluency". The variety of complexity-accuracy-fluency measures makes comparisons hard — [PDF](https://www.hawaii.edu/sls/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Suzuki.pdf)
- How well planning works depends on learner proficiency (collaborative-planning literature) — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/25296590/_2020_Collaborative_Pre_Task_Planning_Processes_and_Second_Language_Performance)
- A 2025 Chinese-language meta-analysis exists (Geng & Li, *Modern Foreign Languages* 48(1), 103–115). I did not retrieve its findings — [PolyU record](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/%E4%BB%BB%E5%8A%A1%E5%87%86%E5%A4%87%E5%9C%A8%E4%BA%8C%E8%AF%AD%E5%8F%A3%E8%AF%AD%E4%B8%AD%E4%BD%9C%E7%94%A8%E7%9A%84%E5%85%83%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90/)
- **Grade: Moderate to strong.**

#### D. Shadowing and elicited imitation
- **No meta-analysis of shadowing was found.** An Oxford MSc dissertation (year not confirmed) systematically reviewed 44 shadowing studies on L2 pronunciation, using narrative synthesis. Its conclusions are "tempered by a number of methodological issues":
  - Studies relied on controlled speaking tasks.
  - They rarely linked acoustic measurements to real listener judgments.
  - Learner-perception studies were mostly surveys.
  - Source — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2)
- Narrative review, *RELC Journal*, "Shadowing: What is it? How to use it. Where will it go?". It suggests beginners start with shadowing *for listening* before moving to shadowing *for speaking* — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380)
- 2016 · quasi-experiment · L2 English · **JP-L1** (43 Japanese undergraduates at two proficiency levels). Hamada (Language Teaching Research) found shadowing works mainly for **lower-proficiency listeners**. It builds bottom-up word recognition (secondary) — [APJSFLE 2017 (citing)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-017-0039-6); [TESL Ontario article](https://contact.teslontario.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Hamada-Shadowing.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance (citing Hamada 2016):
  - A noticeable change came from **two 10–15-minute sessions a week for a month**.
  - Learners should understand the content first, then focus on sound (phonology) rather than meaning while shadowing.
  - Shadowing is cognitively demanding.
  - Sources — [TESL Ontario](https://contact.teslontario.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Hamada-Shadowing.pdf); [APJSFLE](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-017-0039-6)
- **★JFL.** Sakaue (Purdue dissertation, autonomous shadowing practice; listed among Japanese-learner studies in search results, not independently verified):
  - **No significant gains in speed or repair fluency** on read-aloud or storytelling tasks.
  - In storytelling, **silent pauses inside sentence units decreased**, which suggests more native-like pausing.
  - Source — [Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI30506257)
- **★JFL.** A 2015 study in the *Journal of the Phonetic Society of Japan* (19(3)) used native-speaker ratings and suggests shadowing **improves mora-timed rhythm** (the mapping of this finding to this record is a search-summary attribution) — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/onseikenkyu/19/3/19_KJ00010220645/_article/-char/ja)
- **★JFL cluster** of small studies (each finding comes from a search summary; I did not confirm which URL holds which study):
  - Sonu (2018): learners produced a broad range of pitch (F0) movements after shadowing, suggesting Japanese intonation can be learned this way.
  - Guo (2014): 8 Chinese-L1 learners over 3 months, on special morae (geminate consonants and long vowels).
  - Fukuda & Iwashita (2010): 3 advanced learners over a year, tracked through journals and mispronunciation counts.
  - Xu: 3 intermediate learners. Error counts fell, but learners **struggled to keep up regular practice and motivation**.
  - Sources — [J-STAGE, *Journal of Japanese Language Education Methods*](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jlem/24/2/24_44/_article/-char/en); [Hiroshima University bulletin](https://ir.lib.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/journals/BullGradSchEducHU-Part2/i/59/item/31048)
- **Grade: Limited.** The most consistent benefits are for listening and prosody (rhythm, intonation, pausing). Evidence for broad speaking-fluency gains is weak. No evidence was found for *elicited imitation* as a training method (see Gaps).

#### E. Self-recording and self-transcription
- Foundational line of work: Lynch (2001, ELT Journal, "Seeing what they meant: transcribing as a route to noticing"; 2007) and Mennim (2003, 2012, ELT Journal). Learners record a task, transcribe it, correct their own transcript, and then the teacher reformulates it. Later studies build on this — [Salas 2015, *Colombian Applied Linguistics Journal*](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)
- 2001 · small classroom study · L2 English (EAP). In Lynch 2001, **72% of student-made changes were correct**, and students made more changes than the teacher (secondary, unverified) — [Salas 2015](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)
- 2015 · classroom study · L2 English (20 Chilean English-teacher trainees). Salas found results less positive than the earlier literature: students noticed **only about 25%** of what there was to notice (CALJ 17(2), 260–275) — [PDF](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/download/4989/10682/43671)
- **Grade: Limited.** The studies are small and from classrooms, with mixed results. Learners spot only some of their own errors.

#### F. Formulaic sequences (chunks) and fluency
- 2006 · experiment · L2 English (32 Belgian college English majors). Boers, Eyckmans, Kappel, Stengers & Demecheleer (LTR 10(3), 245–261) made the experimental group (n = 17) aware of standard word combinations, while the control group (n = 15) had traditional grammar-versus-vocabulary teaching. Two blind judges rated the experimental group as **more proficient**, and counts of formulaic sequences tracked the ratings — [Antwerp repository](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/59173); [catalog abstract](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c459t-b8541d38e0755f6bd2d5db45834f6af24946a834b346ec994d2f8e83cf83835c3)
- 2009 · case study · L2 English · **JP-L1** (one learner). Wood (Canadian Journal of Applied Linguistics) reports a **strong increase in fluency** (rate of speech, length of runs) after **6 weeks** of focused instruction in formulaic sequences — [CJAL](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/view/19898)
- 2017 · classroom study · L2 English. McGuire & Larson-Hall (TESL Canada Journal) note that the empirical evidence that formulaic sequences improve L2 oral fluency "is still small" — [TESL Canada](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1271/1106)
- A *Language Teaching* article calls for replications of Wood (2009) and Boers et al. (2006), since both are often cited but small — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/8D7676F9CA677D380F6B3870CE20DA9F)
- 2020 · correlational study · L2 English (56 test-takers on the TEEP speaking test). Tavakoli & Uchihara (Language Learning 70(2), 506–547) found:
  - High-frequency n-grams went with a **faster articulation rate**.
  - A higher proportion of n-grams went with **fewer mid-clause pauses**.
  - Stronger word associations went with more pauses at clause ends and **fewer repairs**.
  - Test-takers borrowed n-grams from the task instructions.
  - Source — [CentAUR (Reading)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/86157)
- 2019 · experiment · L2 English · **JP-L1** (24 learners). Reading the source text aloud before an oral summary increased word overlap with the source text and indirectly improved speed and repair fluency, possibly by helping learners chunk multiword sequences — [Lancaster research portal](https://www.research.lancs.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-effects-of-readaloud-assistance-on-second-language-oral-fluency-in-text-summary-speech(42c0c71e-8869-4a6f-a650-db4138b9725c).html)
- **Grade: Limited to moderate.** Correlational evidence linking chunks to fluency is consistent. Intervention studies are few and small.

#### G. Retrieval practice and productive (recall) vocabulary training
- Experiment · L2 vocabulary. A paired-associate study in IRAL (probably Nakata 2016, "Effects of retrieval formats") compared recognition, recall, hybrid and productive-recall-only conditions. **Recall formats beat recognition for productive knowledge** (attribution from a search summary) — [De Gruyter (IRAL)](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- 2016 · small experiment · **JP-L1** (18 Japanese undergraduates). **Productive retrieval practice was significantly more effective on a productive test**. Receptive retrieval matched it only on a receptive test (attribution from a search summary) — [KATE Journal (J-STAGE)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/katejournal/30/0/30_11/_article)
- Direction depends on proficiency. L2→L1 practice worked better for lower-proficiency learners and L1→L2 for higher-proficiency learners (attribution from a search summary) — [Benjamins (ITL)](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/itl.00007.web)
- Further studies exist but I did not retrieve their results: Nakata (2017, SSLA) on repeated retrieval within one session ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)) and Barcroft (2007, Language Learning 57) on opportunities for word retrieval.
- **Grade: Strong for vocabulary retention in general** (see the spacing meta-analysis in Section 5). Evidence that it transfers to *spoken* fluency is indirect.

#### H. Pronunciation instruction, perception training, and automatic speech recognition (ASR) feedback
- 2015 · meta-analysis · 86 studies, mostly L2 English. Lee, Jang & Plonsky (Applied Linguistics 36(3); one search summary wrongly lists the venue as Language Learning) found a **medium overall effect, d = 0.80 [95% CI .77, .81]**, against comparison groups with no pronunciation-focused instruction (secondary, as cited by Saito & Plonsky 2019). The reports included theses and unpublished manuscripts, and intervention length was examined as a moderator — [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-pronunciation-instruction-a-/); [Saito & Plonsky 2019 PDF (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10068780/1/LL2019.pdf); [Dergipark review](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3943948)
- 2019 · meta-analysis · 77 studies (1982–2017). Saito & Plonsky (Language Learning 69(3), 652–708) propose classifying outcome measures as global or specific, human-rated or acoustic, and controlled or spontaneous.
  - Explicit instruction is generally effective.
  - It looks clearly effective on **specific features** such as particular consonants and vowels, but **less clear on global judgments** such as overall accentedness.
  - The two summaries differ slightly in wording.
  - The practical advice is to focus on features that affect whether speech is understood, and to combine controlled practice with meaning-oriented, conversational activities.
  - Sources — [OASIS summary (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10156232/1/OASIS_Summary_Saito_and_Plonsky_2019.pdf); [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/effects-of-second-language-pronunciation-teaching-revisited-a-pro/)
- 2018 · meta-analysis · 25 years of perception-training research. Sakai & Moorman (Applied Psycholinguistics 39(1), 187–224):
  - Tightly controlled perception (listening) training improved **perception by d = 0.92** and **production by d = 0.54**.
  - The correlation between perception gains and production gains was small to medium and not significant.
  - Source — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/57401D28450902EE96659AD10AA11488)
- Follow-up meta-analyses exist, but I did not retrieve their effect sizes:
  - High-variability phonetic training (HVPT, training with many different voices) and production: Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2024, Applied Psycholinguistics, 31 studies) — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7)
  - HVPT and perception: a 2025 SSLA meta-analysis of 79 studies — [Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/S0272263125100879a.pdf/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies.pdf)
- 2024 · meta-analysis · 15 studies (38 effect sizes, 2008–2021), English as a second or foreign language. Ngo, Chen & Lai (ReCALL 36(1), 4–21) found ASR-based pronunciation practice had a **medium effect, g = 0.69**.
  - Effects were stronger for individual sounds (segmentals) than for prosody (suprasegmentals).
  - Practicing **with peers beat practicing alone**.
  - **Explicit corrective feedback produced large effects**, and indirect feedback such as dictation produced moderate ones.
  - Source — [Cambridge (ReCALL)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)
- Consensus: several syntheses show pronunciation instruction works — [Nagle & Hiver replication paper (Language Teaching)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/295A0BD9437FFB55980836BE9EAA3520/S0261444823000083a.pdf/optimizing-second-language-pronunciation-instruction-replications-of-martin-and-sippel-2021-olson-and-offerman-2021-and-thomson-2012.pdf)
- **★JFL pronunciation-training studies** (all small):
  - Hirata (2002, ICSLP): production training *with visual feedback* on Japanese pitch and length contrasts. I did not retrieve the results — [ISCA archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2002/hirata02_icslp.html)
  - A 1995 ICPhS pilot trained 10 English speakers on Japanese vowel and consonant length and pitch accent with computer visual feedback. Results were mixed, "but visual feedback proved its utility" — [ICPhS 1995](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_1/p13.1_330.pdf)
  - Motohashi-Saigo & Hardison (2009, *Language Learning & Technology* 13(2)): waveform-based audiovisual training significantly improved production of geminates (double consonants), especially in the audiovisual group — [ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/44179/1/13_02_motohashisaigohardison.pdf)
  - Hirata & Kelly: adding hand gestures to speech did *not* improve perception of short versus long vowels compared with speech alone (attribution from a search summary) — [Colgate](https://colgate.edu/media/12211/download)
  - Hirata (2024) compared auditory, visual and gestural pitch-accent training — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/AB2195C963F348823C8175220F9F9EA1/core-reader)
- **Grade: Strong** for explicit instruction on specific features in controlled tasks. **Moderate** for transfer to global intelligibility in spontaneous speech. **Moderate** for ASR feedback (English only). **Limited** for Japanese-specific training.

#### I. Communication-strategy training (interactional competence)
- 1995 · quasi-experiment, 6 weeks · L2 English (109 Hungarian EFL students). Dörnyei (TESOL Quarterly 29(1), 55–85) trained topic avoidance and replacement, circumlocution, and fillers. Instruction altered how often and how well students used the strategies, but it appeared to have **little overall effect on task performance** (secondary) — [secondary summary (Tsukuba)](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/40997/files/JACTAT%20Bruno.pdf)
- 2005 · quasi-experiment · L2 English · **JP-L1** (62 Japanese female EFL learners). Nakatani (MLJ) used awareness-raising training on oral communication strategies. The training group **significantly improved oral proficiency test scores**, while the control group did not. Raised awareness did not necessarily turn into strategy *use* in the short term, so learners need ample chances to use strategies in spontaneous talk (secondary) — [NUFS paper (citing)](https://www.nufs.ac.jp/media/Junko2018.pdf)
- 2011 · meta-analysis of general strategy instruction (not specific to speaking) · mixed L2s. Plonsky (Language Learning 61(4)) found strategy instruction effective, moderated by the type and number of strategies, second- vs. foreign-language context, and intervention length. I did not retrieve the overall d — [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-strategy-instruction-a-meta-/)
- The literature on teaching oral communication strategies is "rather mixed", and there is a "paucity of work" — [TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1056/875/1137)
- **★JFL.** Ohta 2001 proposes a **six-stage developmental model of L2 listener responses**, the back-channel reactions such as *aizuchi*, based on adult Japanese learners — [LINGUIST List review](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/linguist/2001-May/013397.html)
- **Grade: Limited to moderate.**

#### J. Reading aloud
- 2019 · experiment · L2 English · **JP-L1** (24 learners): reading aloud before an oral summary indirectly improved speed and repair fluency (see section F) — [Lancaster](https://www.research.lancs.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-effects-of-readaloud-assistance-on-second-language-oral-fluency-in-text-summary-speech(42c0c71e-8869-4a6f-a650-db4138b9725c).html)
- Master's thesis · L2 Russian (BYU). Daily repeated reading aloud produced a significant fluency difference, "but because of the high attrition rate and short duration … cannot be generalized" — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/3325)
- Classroom study · L2 English. Repeated reading aloud with a partner who gives feedback "may be a useful instructional strategy to improve English pronunciation and fluency" — [UAB](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/jtl3/jtl3_a2013m2-3v6n1/jtl3_a2013m2-3v6n1p35.pdf)
- Reading aloud as an *assessment* tool · **JP-L1** (63 secondary-school students). Articulation rate and pauses inside clauses predicted perceived fluency — [JALT Journal](https://jalt-publications.org/articles/28081-research-forum%C2%A0-relationship-between-japanese-efl-learners%E2%80%99-perceived-fluency-and)
- **Grade: Limited.**

#### K. Self-talk and private speech
- 2001 · longitudinal classroom case study, one year · **★JFL** (7 adult learners of Japanese at the University of Washington, 1996–97). Ohta defines private speech as "audible speech not adapted to an addressee", captured with personal microphones.
  - Private speech served **repetition, manipulation, and "vicarious response"**: answering silently or under one's breath to questions addressed to others.
  - It was a site of creative manipulation of the language and hypothesis testing.
  - The book was the first to study adult learners' private speech in foreign-language classes.
  - Sources — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Second-Language-Acquisition-Processes-in-the-Classroom-Learning-Japanese/Ohta/p/book/9780805838015); [LINGUIST List review](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/linguist/2001-May/013397.html); [TESL-EJ review](https://www.cc.kyoto-su.ac.jp/information/tesl-ej/ej19/r13.html)
- **Grade: Limited.** The work is descriptive, and no intervention study of deliberate self-talk was found.

#### L. Conversation with dialogue systems and chatbots (evidence for the "AI partner" mode)
- 2022 · meta-analysis · 17 studies, 100 effect sizes, mixed L2s. Bibauw et al. (*Language Learning & Technology*) found dialogue systems had a **medium effect on L2 proficiency, d = 0.58** — [LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1035)
- 2025 · three-level meta-analysis · 16 studies, 89 effect sizes. Hou & Min (ReCALL) found a **moderate effect on speaking, g = 0.61**. It was moderated by system type, constraints on meaning, and modality (spoken or written). **Most systems were rule-based, not generative AI** — [Cambridge (ReCALL)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B)
- 2026 · meta-analysis · 17 chatbot-and-speaking studies. Kansizoglu & Kayhan found g = 1.035. This is an outlier against the other estimates, so check its inclusion criteria — [Dergipark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/5297158)
- 2026 · meta-analysis (EUROCALL presentation, not yet published) · 67 studies, 237 effect sizes. Wang, Bibauw, Noreillie & Desmet found **g = 0.61 overall** and **g = 0.65 for productive skills**. Delayed post-tests (g = 0.92) exceeded immediate ones (g = 0.58) — [UCLouvain record ("Hype or hope?")](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/entities/publication/29791a54-111b-40eb-bacb-346494980619)
- **Grade: Moderate** that dialogue-system practice helps speaking (about g ≈ 0.6). The evidence for modern LLM voice partners specifically is thin. Most studies are English-L2, and many predate generative AI.

**Effect-size caveats for the report.**
- Laboratory studies produce larger effects than classrooms (Li 2010; Mackey & Goo).
- Controlled outcome measures, such as reading word lists aloud, overstate gains compared with spontaneous speech (Saito & Plonsky 2019).
- Lyster & Saito found adults benefit less from CF than younger learners.
- Most participants were university EFL students in short interventions, so effects for a self-directed adult learning Japanese are an extrapolation.

**Trade-off rule** (from Thai & Boers 2016 and Johnson & Abdi Tabari 2022).
- Time pressure → fluency.
- Planning time → fluency and complexity.
- Unpressured or no time limit → accuracy and complexity.
- A practice cycle should therefore alternate conditions rather than always speaking as fast as possible.

**How to apply each technique.** This table is my inference for the learner profile: a Vietnamese software engineer in Vietnam, JLPT N4–N3, aiming at IT-engineer interviews and workplace talk such as self-introductions, explaining past projects, technical explanations, and stand-up meetings.

| Technique | Alone | With a tutor | With an AI partner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task repetition, 4/3/2 | Record the same job-relevant monologue (self-intro, "a project I built", "how I fixed a bug") in 4, 3, then 2 minutes. Next day, do one constant-time version focused on accuracy (Thai & Boers). Re-perform after about a week. | Reuse the same mock-interview questions across sessions with new follow-up questions (procedural repetition). | Have the AI act as a timed interviewer that repeats the task and adds follow-ups. After round 3, ask for a transcript and an error list. |
| Planning | 1–5 minutes of keyword notes (not a full script) before each task. Do some tasks with no time limit for accuracy. | Ask for a minute to plan before role-plays. | Get the task prompt ahead of time and plan from notes. |
| Corrective feedback | (Not applicable alone; use self-transcription.) | Ask the tutor to *prompt* (elicit, ask for clarification, give a metalinguistic clue) on forms you have studied for JLPT, and to *recast* new or complex forms. Feedback at the end of an activity is acceptable, since timing evidence shows no clear difference. Keep an error log. | Tell the AI to prompt first, then give the correct form plus a one-line rule. Collect errors into spaced-review cards. |
| Self-transcription | Transcribe 1–2 minutes of your recording and correct it yourself. | Tutor checks the corrected transcript, because learners catch only some of their errors (Salas: about 25%). | AI does the speech-to-text and checks your corrections. Verify the AI's transcription accuracy (see Gaps). |
| Shadowing | 10–15 minutes, 2–4 times a week, only on audio you already understand. Focus on rhythm, intonation and pausing. Expect listening and prosody gains more than fluency gains. | Not needed. | Use generated, level-appropriate scripts with natural text-to-speech audio. |
| Formulaic sequences | Keep a phrase bank of interview and workplace chunks. Drill them by productive recall (meaning → say aloud), then use them in 4/3/2 talks. | Ask the tutor to supply natural chunks when they recast you. | Ask the AI to extract reusable chunks from its native-like rewrite of your transcript. |
| Productive retrieval | Spaced-repetition cards in the L1/English → spoken Japanese direction: say it aloud before flipping. | Oral quizzing. | Oral quizzing. |
| Pronunciation | Perception training on long vs. short vowels, geminates and pitch accent, with pitch or waveform visualization (★JFL studies). | Focus on features that affect comprehensibility (Saito & Plonsky). | ASR feedback (g = 0.69 in English), but ASR accuracy on accented Japanese is unknown. Practicing with a peer beat practicing alone. |

- The original 4/3/2 sources, Maurice (1983) and Nation (1989, *System*), were not retrieved.

- Studies I could not retrieve:
  - Kadota's shadowing books (2007/2019).
  - Foote & McDonough (2017), on shadowing with mobile devices.

  - Barcroft (2007) and Nakata (2017) results.
- **Elicited imitation as a training technique:** no evidence was found. It is mainly used to *measure* proficiency.
- **Deliberate self-talk interventions:** none were found.

## 3. Speaking anxiety, willingness to communicate (WTC), and interventions for learners with few chances to speak

### Takeaway
Language anxiety has a moderate negative relationship with achievement (r ≈ −.36). Willingness to communicate depends most on *perceived* communicative competence, which is a stronger predictor than anxiety or motivation. The intervention evidence is weaker: a narrative systematic review of classroom interventions, plus a newer, mixed body of chatbot studies. Most chatbot studies report anxiety reductions, but the effect varies by learner.

### Cited Findings
- 2019 · meta-analysis · 97 reports, 105 samples, N = 19,933, 23 countries, mixed L2s. Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky (SSLA 41(2), 363–387) found a mean **r = −.36** between L2 anxiety and achievement across 216 correlations, about 13% of variance. The relationship was moderated by the type of achievement measure, educational level, target language and type of anxiety. The authors call this "firm evidence" for anxiety's negative role — [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement/); [presentation slides](https://www.slideshare.net/multiolelo/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement-a-metaanalysis)
- 2019 · meta-analysis · mixed L2s. Shirvan, Khajavy, MacIntyre & Taherian (*Journal of Psycholinguistic Research* 48, 1241–1267) found that perceived communicative competence, language anxiety and motivation are all moderately correlated with L2 WTC. **Perceived competence had the largest effect** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10936-019-09656-9)
- 2022 · meta-analytic structural equation modeling · 44 samples, N = 12,094. Jin & Lee found **perceived L2 competence influenced WTC most** of the three high-evidence factors. Proficiency and context (inside vs. outside the classroom) moderated the relationships — [SAGE figshare](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/Willingness_to_Communicate_and_its_High-Evidence_Factors_A_Meta-Analytic_Structural_Equation_Modeling_Approach/5950697)
- 2024 · meta-analysis · 50 studies. Lou et al. (*Contemporary Foreign Languages Studies*) found a medium negative correlation between WTC and L2 anxiety, **r = −0.343** — [SJTU journal](https://www.qk.sjtu.edu.cn/cfls/EN/abstract/article/1674-8921/47759)
- 2023 · correlational, mediation model · L2 not confirmed (likely English). Testing impostor feelings against WTC, the effect was **fully mediated by perceived competence** rather than communication anxiety — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9869140/)
- 1998 · theory, heuristic model. MacIntyre, Clément, Dörnyei & Noels (MLJ) propose a **pyramid model** of WTC.
  - Actual L2 use sits at the top, then the intention to speak, then the desire to communicate with a specific person together with momentary self-confidence. Enduring influences form the lower layers.
  - **WTC in the L1 does not necessarily transfer to the L2.**
  - The layer labels come partly from secondary sources.
  - Sources — [original PDF (Alberta)](https://sites.psych.ualberta.ca/IClab/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Cl--mentMacIntyreDoernyeiNoels1998.pdf); [Wikipedia: WTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willingness_to_communicate)
- 2024 · book. Henry & MacIntyre revise this into a 3D pyramid model based on complexity theory — [OAPEN open-access book](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/109796/1/9781800411951.pdf)
- 2021 · systematic review with narrative synthesis. Toyama & Yamazaki (*Frontiers in Psychology*), described as the first systematic review of educational interventions for foreign-language anxiety.
  - It groups intervention features into student–student interaction, student–teacher interaction, **self-management**, and **mood boosters**, delivered individually or interactionally.
  - Studies varied too much in type, duration and anxiety scale to pool statistically.
  - Earlier (pre-2000) advice included positive self-talk and talking about one's feelings with someone.
  - Source — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.614184/full)
- A newer critical review of anxiety-reduction interventions from the past decade exists. I did not retrieve its findings — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/CC3A2FCBE267D90DEC8131A5D0962472/core-reader)
- **AI chatbots and speaking anxiety** (2024–2026, mostly Chinese learners of English):
  - Wang et al. (2025, *System* 127): an experiment with a control group and two chatbot groups (33 students each), measuring WTC, speaking anxiety and self-perceived competence. It highlights the value of human-like avatars — [XJTLU record](https://scholar.xjtlu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-different-conversational-generative-ai-chatbots-on-)
  - Wang (IRAL): 103 learners comparing AI with teacher and peer practice over three rounds of speaking tests. It also notes the limits of AI-supported instruction — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2024-0310/pdf)
  - Ong (2025, master's thesis, National Chengchi University): 105 anxious learners. Chatbot practice most reduced **fear of negative evaluation**. More human-like chatbots → more trust → higher WTC, but trust did not directly reduce anxiety — [NCCU](https://nccur.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/158726)
  - A 2026 review of 2022–2025 studies finds that the effect "depends on the individual". Some studies show no effect or even side effects, while **most report significant anxiety reductions, d = 0.39–1.52** — [SHS Web of Conferences](https://www.shs-conferences.org/articles/shsconf/pdf/2026/09/shsconf_icepcc2026_02014.pdf)
  - 2026 · structural equation modeling · 350 Chinese undergraduates. Enjoyment and anxiety had modest effects on WTC with generative AI. **Grit** had stronger direct and indirect effects. The search summary did not make clear which of these two records reports these numbers — [PMC: "Drivers of WTC with generative AI"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12883774/); [Frontiers in Psychology 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1754495/full)
- ASR pronunciation practice worked better **with peers than alone** (Ngo, Chen & Lai 2024) — [Cambridge (ReCALL)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)

### Inferences
- **Treat the problem as "perceived competence", not only "anxiety".** The strongest lever for WTC in the meta-analyses is how competent the learner *feels*. For someone with no daily speaking, competence-building rehearsal is therefore a plausible anxiety intervention: scripted-then-unscripted repetition of the exact talks needed (self-introduction, project explanation, technical Q&A). Thai & Boers and de Jong & Perfetti show that fluency rises quickly on repeated content. This mechanism is my inference; no study tested it directly as an anxiety intervention.
- **Graduated exposure ladder** (inference built on the chatbot evidence and Toyama & Yamazaki's categories):
  1. Private speech and solo recordings.
  2. AI partner, where there is no human listener to fear judgment from, which matches the "fear of negative evaluation" finding.
  3. A paid tutor (a supportive student–teacher interaction).
  4. Peers, language exchanges, mock interviews.
  5. Real interviews.
- Self-management techniques such as positive self-talk appear in the review, but their individual effectiveness isn't quantified.
- **Caveat.** The chatbot-anxiety literature is new and mostly Chinese EFL, has mixed results, and includes a thesis and conference-level work. It should be presented as promising, not established.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986), the FLCAS: its three components (communication apprehension, test anxiety, fear of negative evaluation), its items, and its reliability. The search budget was exhausted.
- I found no study of speaking anxiety or WTC among **★JFL** learners, or among Vietnamese learners of Japanese.
- I found no meta-analysis that pools effect sizes for anxiety-*reduction* interventions. Toyama & Yamazaki is narrative.

## 4. Is there evidence for delaying speaking (a "silent period"), or that speaking early fossilizes errors?

### Takeaway
No adult evidence was found that early speaking causes fossilized errors. The best comparative evidence (Shintani et al. 2013) shows production-based practice is needed for durable *productive* knowledge, and CF studies show learners' errors remain changeable. There is some older, limited evidence that a comprehension-first start can help pronunciation. The silent period is a concept mostly documented in children, and even there it is contested. For a learner already at N4–N3, the question is moot: the evidence says start producing now, with feedback.

### Cited Findings
- The silent period is described mainly in **children**. There is controversy over whether a defined silent period exists. Critics cite variable methods and definitions of silence, and a lack of evidence that the silence involves active learning rather than incomprehension or shyness — [Wikipedia: Silent period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_period)
- 2019 · study of early-years children. Some children did enter a silent period, and **shyness was a risk factor for it being prolonged**. The authors argue that normalizing it may infringe children's right to participate — [Teanga (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/5894f271c6e8414fa944ffd4aabb551d)
- 1995 · conference paper (ICPhS) · foreign-language learners. Barrera Pousada reviews comprehension-first approaches, citing Postovsky and others. Learners **whose oral production was delayed improved more in pronunciation** than learners who produced from the start. I did not verify the sample or design — [ICPhS 1995](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_1/p13.1_270.pdf)
- Postovsky's work on the priority of aural comprehension is cited throughout the comprehension-first literature, for example by the 1995 ICPhS paper above. His 1981 chapter, "The priority of aural comprehension in the language acquisition process" (in Winitz, ed., *The Comprehension Approach to Foreign Language Instruction*), appeared in search results only as a reference and was not retrieved — [ICPhS 1995](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_1/p13.1_270.pdf)
- 2013 · meta-analysis. Production-based instruction outperformed comprehension-based instruction on **delayed tests of productive knowledge**. Comprehension-based instruction's advantage for receptive knowledge faded by the delayed tests — [Curtin](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/54469)
- Theory plus immersion evidence: Swain's Output Hypothesis came from work with Canadian immersion students who had extensive comprehensible input. She argued that production, not input alone, forces attention to grammar — [Wikipedia: Comprehensible output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)
- Theory: practice effects are skill-specific (DeKeyser) — [Cambridge excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/84040/excerpt/9780521684040_excerpt.pdf)
- CF effects persist to long-delayed post-tests (Lyster & Saito 2010), and beginning JFL learners' errors responded to implicit negative feedback within the study period (Iwashita 2003). Interlanguage errors are therefore not "locked in" early — [Cambridge (SSLA)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1); [Iwashita 2003](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/negative-feedback-and-positive-evidence-in-taskbased-interaction/7A1B2BADFD35078D677D98C6D81569B6)
- Adult classroom silence is more often a problem than a strategy. A 2022 thesis found that adult learners' silent period "was not treated by their teachers due to 'pedagogical barriers'" — [USQ thesis (Ekici)](https://research.usq.edu.au/download/7d65c47c365823af7a9132a57bc0f28f186fd6586ea22229d5b92803c36e7443/13758505/Gullu%20Ekici%20-%20Thesis.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Current consensus (my synthesis):**
  - Input-rich exposure matters, and a short listening-heavy start may help perception and pronunciation, but that evidence is old and limited.
  - Deliberately *withholding* speaking is not supported for adults.
  - The idea that early speaking "fossilizes" errors has no empirical support in what I found. Fossilization research concerns long-term stabilization, not early production.
  - The responsiveness of errors to feedback is evidence against the "lock-in" claim.
- **For this learner** (already at N4–N3, with years of input-based study), the practical risk is the opposite: long silence has left the knowledge declarative. The evidence favors immediate pushed output with feedback, plus perception training for Japanese-specific sound contrasts such as vowel length, geminates and pitch accent (Section 2H).
- Popular "no speaking for N hours" methods (for example, ALG-style approaches) should be labeled **practitioner opinion**. No peer-reviewed adult studies supporting them were retrieved.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Postovsky (1974, MLJ), the classic adult experiment on delaying oral practice, which studied L2 Russian at the Defense Language Institute. The search budget ran out.
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of ALG or "comprehensible-input-only" programs for adults.
- I found no direct study of whether early adult production leads to fossilization.
- I did not retrieve Krashen's primary texts on the silent period.

## 5. How should practice time be allocated? Frequency, spacing, and minimum effective dose

### Takeaway
Spacing practice beats massing it, with a medium-to-large effect in an L2 meta-analysis. Most of that evidence is vocabulary and grammar, not speaking. At the program level, concentrated (intensive) schedules of the same total hours did as well or better for adults. Nation's framework gives roughly equal time to four strands. **No study establishes a minimum effective dose for speaking practice**, so only dosages used in individual studies can be reported.

### Cited Findings
- 2022 · meta-analysis · 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411. Kim & Webb (Language Learning 72(1), 269–319):
  - **Spacing had a medium-to-large effect** on L2 learning.
  - Shorter and longer gaps were equal on immediate tests, but **shorter gaps were less effective on delayed tests**.
  - Equal and expanding gaps were statistically equivalent.
  - Results varied with the learning target, **number of sessions**, type of practice, activity, feedback timing, and retention interval.
  - Sources — [BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001); [Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/en/paper-details/the-effects-of-spaced-practice-on-second-language-learning-a-meta-analysis/813033326355939328-6763)
- A secondary citation gives **g = 0.80 on delayed post-tests** for spaced vs. massed practice. Another paper describes the same meta-analysis's effect as "small to medium". These are conflicting characterizations, probably of different outcomes — [Leeds/White Rose manuscript](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807)
- Thesis · vocabulary. Spaced practice equaled massed practice on immediate tests for sentence production and flashcards, but was **more effective on delayed tests** — [Western University thesis (Kim)](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)
- A 2022 state-of-the-art review in *Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching* confirms spacing's role in L2 practice and calls for more research. A 2026 study in the same journal replicates a finding that contradicts the usual spacing advantage — [SSLLT 2022](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/35602/30649/76142); [SSLLT 2026](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/49487/44378/139556)
- 2019 · theory (coda article). Suzuki, Nakata & DeKeyser's **Desirable Difficulty Framework** (MLJ 103(3), 713–720) says optimal L2 practice must balance three sources of difficulty: practice conditions (such as spacing), linguistic difficulty, and learner differences — [Zendy (DOI)](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/modl.12585); [UB PDF](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/216425/1/245169.pdf)
- Lag effects for grammar are **inconsistent**: some studies favor longer gaps, others shorter, others find little difference — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/A9E9F6888901DBF1F2D7B52EC084C010/core-reader)
- 2007 · quasi-experiment · adult EFL in Spain. Serrano & Muñoz, "Same hours, different time distribution": 110 hours spread over 7 months, over 3–4 months, or packed into 5 summer weeks. **Concentrated instruction was more beneficial** than distributing the hours across many months — [UB repository](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/457c4ebe-8087-4511-9f7a-044a352b2fd2)
- 2010 · quasi-experiment · adult EFL, oral production. Serrano (VIAL) found **no significant differences in oral gains** between schedules, only a slight tendency favoring intensive instruction — [UB repository](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/handle/2445/164085)
- Serrano's state-of-the-art review: "very little research" compares massed and spaced grammar instruction — [UB](https://diposit.ub.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/911f8da8-9666-4917-9411-c9ae6754cd7b/content)
- Four strands: roughly equal time each, with language-focused learning at no more than about 25% — [Nation 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/357330216.pdf); [Wikipedia: Paul Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nation)
- Feedback timing: immediate vs. delayed CF shows no clear difference — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1026174/full)
- The interval between task repetitions is an under-reported design variable, and Rogers' systematic review is devoted to it — [PolyU](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/113277/1/Rogers_Time_Between_Tasks.pdf)
- **Dosages used in individual studies** (not minimum doses):
  - Shadowing: two 10–15-minute sessions a week for a month (Hamada 2016, via a practitioner article) — [TESL Ontario](https://contact.teslontario.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Hamada-Shadowing.pdf)
  - Formulaic-sequence instruction: 6 weeks (Wood 2009) — [CJAL](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/view/19898)
  - Communication-strategy training: 6 weeks (Dörnyei 1995) — [secondary](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/40997/files/JACTAT%20Bruno.pdf)
  - 4/3/2: three deliveries of the same talk in one sitting (Thai & Boers 2016) — [record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/tesq.232)

### Inferences
- **The two strands of evidence fit together.**
  - Laboratory spacing concerns re-encountering the *same item* after a gap.
  - Intensive-program studies concern *total weekly hours and continuity*.
  - A self-directed learner can do both: practice **daily**, which is intensive at the week level, while **revisiting each specific item or talk after growing gaps**, such as the next day, the following week, and two to three weeks later.
- **Suggested time budget.** This is inference; it applies Nation and the meta-analyses but has not itself been tested. Assuming about 6–8 hours a week alongside JLPT N3 preparation:
  - **Meaning-focused input, about 25%.** JLPT listening and reading, plus IT-domain listening such as tech talks and meeting recordings.
  - **Language-focused learning, at most about 25%.** JLPT grammar and vocabulary, pronunciation and perception drills, error-log review.
  - **Meaning-focused output, about 25%.** 2–3 tutor sessions a week (task-based, mock interviews, with prompts-first feedback) plus AI conversation.
  - **Fluency development, about 25%.** Daily 10–20 minutes of 4/3/2 on job talks, shadowing on understood audio, and productive-recall drills said aloud.
  - Because JLPT study is entirely input and language-focused, the learner will need to *protect* the two output strands deliberately.
- **Dose.** Without minimum-dose data, report session lengths from the studies (10–15 minutes for shadowing, 6-week courses) and say plainly that the optimal weekly dose of speaking practice is unknown. Kim & Webb found that the number of sessions moderates the spacing effect, which supports many short sessions over few long ones. That is an inference beyond what was measured.

### Gaps
- **No research was found on a minimum effective dose** of speaking practice, or on how often tutoring sessions should happen.
- I found no spacing study that used *spoken fluency* as the outcome. The best evidence is vocabulary and grammar.
- I did not retrieve Rogers' recommended repetition intervals or the findings of the MDPI 2023 study on task-repetition schedules (*Languages* 8(4), 252).
- Exact effect sizes from Kim & Webb (2022) need checking: g = 0.80 delayed is a secondary figure, and another source calls the effect "small to medium".

## 6. How can a learner measure their own speaking progress?

### Takeaway
The best-supported self-measures are timing measures from periodic recordings: speed (speech or articulation rate) and pausing, especially **pauses in the middle of clauses**. These track listeners' fluency judgments most closely. Accuracy and complexity need transcripts. Can-do self-assessments correlate only moderately with external tests (r ≈ .44–.55 for speaking), so they work as a complement rather than a replacement.

### Cited Findings
- 2021 · meta-analysis · 22 studies, 263 effect sizes, mostly L2 English. Suzuki, Kormos & Uchihara (MLJ 105(2), 435–463) found that **speed fluency and breakdown fluency, particularly how often speakers pause mid-clause, were the most useful predictors of perceived fluency**. The strength of the link depended on how speech samples were prepared and on what raters were told to attend to — [Lancaster record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/152963)
- A secondary citation says **speech rate alone explained about 57.8% of the variance** in perceived fluency (verify against the original) — [SSLA 2025 meta-analysis, citing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF27ACC694B94D3B99D8BC6D05BFCA54/S0272263125000014a.pdf/the-relationships-among-l2-fluency-intelligibility-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- A 2025 SSLA meta-analysis exists on the relationships among L2 fluency, intelligibility, comprehensibility and accentedness. I did not retrieve its results — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF27ACC694B94D3B99D8BC6D05BFCA54/S0272263125000014a.pdf/the-relationships-among-l2-fluency-intelligibility-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- 2004 · correlational · L2 English (16 Hungarian speakers, 3 native and 3 non-native teacher raters). Kormos & Dénes (*System* 32(2), 145–164):
  - **Speech rate, phonation-time ratio (share of time spent speaking), stressed words per minute, and mean length of run/utterance** predicted fluency ratings.
  - The **number of filled and unfilled pauses did not**.
  - Raters differed in how much weight they gave accuracy, lexical diversity and mean pause length.
  - Mean length of run is usually measured with a 0.25-second pause cut-off.
  - A later critique notes that with so few participants and strongly interrelated variables, the results are hard to interpret.
  - Sources — [Lancaster record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/10747); [replication study (Language Teaching 2024)](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/06EF64DD4ECA60FDDBEE5062426D6370/S0261444824000120a.pdf/replication-research-in-the-domain-of-perceived-l2-fluency-approximate-and-close-replications-of-kormos-and-denes-2004-and-rossiter-2009.pdf); [Jyväskylä thesis (variable definitions)](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/60156/5/Towards%20the%20phonetic%20basis%20of%20spoken%20second%20language%20assessment.pdf)
- **Conflict to note.** Kormos & Dénes (2004, N = 16) found pause counts did not affect perceptions. The later meta-analysis (2021) singles out *mid-clause* pause frequency. Separating pauses by location probably explains the difference, and the meta-analysis is the stronger evidence — [Lancaster (2021 meta-analysis)](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/152963); [Lancaster (2004)](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/10747)
- Read-aloud fluency:
  - **JP-L1** (63 students): articulation rate and pauses inside clauses predicted perceived fluency — [JALT Journal](https://jalt-publications.org/articles/28081-research-forum%C2%A0-relationship-between-japanese-efl-learners%E2%80%99-perceived-fluency-and)
  - 2025, 57 Slovak students reading in L1 and L2: mid-clause pausing is a promising L2-specific fluency indicator — [EBSCO record (*Language and Speech*)](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/language-and-linguistics/86209e0d-56e9-544b-9c58-19e227f65862/speech-fluency-production-and-perception-in-l1-slovak-and-l2-english-read-speech)
- Fluency is not the same as accent. Pause patterns contributed little to perceived non-native accent in read speech, and proficient L2 speakers can be highly fluent yet clearly accented — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7124187)
- Fluency dimensions used in measurement: **speed, breakdown** (pausing) and **repair** (self-corrections, repetitions), alongside multiword-sequence measures (Tavakoli & Uchihara 2020) — [CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/86157)
- Self-assessment validity (secondary; check the originals):
  - Ross (1998), meta-analysis: self-assessment correlated with other proficiency measures at about .63 overall and **.55 for speaking**, with "considerable variation" in learners' accuracy.
  - Li & Zhang (2021), meta-analysis of 67 studies and 214 correlations: about **.45 overall and .44 for speaking**.
  - Sources — [Butler, *Language Teaching* review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/selfassessment-in-second-language-learning/00D73BD54F6227D484297EEAB8F51BC0); [Winke, Zhang & Pierce (SSLA)](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0272263122000079); [Ross 1998 record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/7b2a66a61b285e56b4d1b66af1ce8770)
- Butler's review gives a timeline of 15 studies (1985–2019) on L2 *speaking* self-assessment and identifies three types of variables that affect its accuracy — [Cambridge (*Language Teaching*)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/selfassessment-in-second-language-learning/00D73BD54F6227D484297EEAB8F51BC0)
- Can-do statement validation (individual studies; no meta-analysis found):
  - Brown, Dewey & Cox (2014): retrospective "then-now" can-do self-assessment — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/5887)
  - Tigchelaar (MSU dissertation): an instrument of 50 NCSSFL-ACTFL (2015) Can-Do Statements for spoken proficiency — [MSU](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/19265/FULL_TEXT/view)
  - Winke, Zhang & Pierce (SSLA): 807 L2-Spanish learners took a computer-adaptive speaking self-assessment alongside the ACTFL computer-based oral proficiency interview (OPIc) — [Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0272263122000079)

### Inferences
**A self-measurement protocol for the report** (my design, built on the measures above):
- **What to record.** Once a month, record 2–3 minutes of the *same* prompt (for example, "explain a system you built") plus one *new* prompt of the same type. The fixed prompt tracks rehearsed performance; the new one tracks transfer.
- **Fluency measures:**
  - **Speech rate**: morae or syllables per second including pauses.
  - **Articulation rate**: the same, excluding pauses.
  - **Mid-clause silent pauses** of 0.25 seconds or more per minute, the best single predictor of perceived fluency.
  - **Mean length of run**: units between pauses of 0.25 seconds or more.
  - **Repairs per minute.**
  - For Japanese, count **morae** rather than syllables. In Japanese, clause boundaries usually fall after conjunctive forms such as the te-form, から or けど, so a "mid-clause" pause is one that falls inside a clause.
  - These Japanese adaptations are my inference; I did not retrieve a JFL source.
- **Accuracy** from a transcript: errors per 100 words or per clause, or the percentage of error-free clauses.
- **Complexity:** clauses per unit, and the variety of grammar and vocabulary.
- **Monthly ratings:** CEFR/JF Standard or ACTFL can-do self-ratings, cross-checked against a tutor's rating, because self-ratings correlate only about .44–.55 with external measures.
- **Expect trade-offs.** Fluency usually improves first (Thai & Boers). Accuracy and complexity require unpressured and planned practice.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve JFL-specific fluency norms (morae per second for native speakers and for learners at JLPT N3/N2) or validated JFL fluency measures. The search budget was exhausted. This also belongs to the Japanese-specific researcher's scope.
- I did not retrieve validation data for the CEFR Companion Volume (2020) or the JF Standard descriptors, nor the ACTFL can-do validity results (Tigchelaar, Bowles, Winke & Gass 2017).
- I found no study that tests self-directed learners' use of periodic recordings as a measurement or motivation tool.
- Practical tools such as Praat pause-detection scripts are not covered by any retrieved source. I found no study that checks automatic fluency measures against human ratings for L2 Japanese.
